# Planning Videos

## Why publish planning videos?
Planning videos explain the reasoning behind a project. Development timelapses show what changed; planning videos explain why the project is being shaped that way. Together they create a fuller development story and help viewers understand the design before they see it implemented.

Planning is not filler between coding videos. A design decision can be a valuable episode when it affects the product, creates an interesting trade-off, or teaches viewers how to think about a real engineering problem.

## Suitable subjects
- Introducing the project vision, problem, and intended users.
- Defining an initial release and separating Core from Pro.
- Designing architecture and major components.
- Exploring UI layouts, navigation, and first-run experience.
- Designing the ATLAS Action Protocol (AAP).
- Comparing possible approaches and explaining trade-offs.
- Planning tool discovery and installation consent.
- Deciding what an AI mode may or may not do.
- Considering repository operations and user-controlled push permissions.
- Reviewing a feature idea and deciding whether it belongs.
- Revisiting an earlier decision after implementation reveals a problem.

Only describe details present in the current ATLAS plan, or label new concepts clearly as proposals.

## Visual material
A planning video can use:
- architecture diagrams and flowcharts;
- UI sketches and mockups;
- notes, specification documents, and feature maps;
- small code excerpts when they clarify an interface or protocol;
- side-by-side comparisons of possible approaches;
- annotated screenshots of the actual planning workspace;
- a narrated slide sequence when the subject benefits from it.

A planning video does not require face-camera footage. The visuals should change as the explanation progresses; avoid showing a static page of text for the entire video.

## Suggested story structure
1. **Hook:** introduce the decision or problem that makes this topic interesting.
2. **Context:** explain where the project is and why the question has appeared.
3. **Requirements:** identify what the design must support and what it must avoid.
4. **Options:** show the plausible approaches, not an artificial list of alternatives.
5. **Trade-offs:** explain what each approach improves, complicates, or leaves unresolved.
6. **Current decision:** state what is selected now and why.
7. **Uncertainty:** identify what still needs testing or creator approval.
8. **Next step:** explain how the plan will be validated or implemented.

The order can change. A short topic may need only a few of these sections.

## Keep it authentic
Show the actual thought process, including ideas that were rejected or changed. It is fine to say that a decision is provisional, that two options remain open, or that implementation may change the design. Do not present uncertain plans as completed engineering.

Avoid turning every small decision into a full video. Combine related decisions when they form one understandable story. Avoid reading a specification line by line; translate it into diagrams, examples, and explanations.

## Relationship to development episodes
Planning and development belong to the same Building ATLAS journey. They can be separate episodes, combined when the story is clearer, or published in whichever order best explains the project. There is no need to alternate perfectly. A planning episode can precede implementation, follow a discovery, or explain why an earlier implementation is being redesigned.
